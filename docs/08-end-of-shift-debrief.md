# End-of-Shift Debrief

**Education only — this field manual is never medical advice.** This chapter covers the last forty minutes: formal handoff to the incoming charge, the thank-down to your team, and the five-minute personal debrief that turns one shift into skill. It does not replace your facility's handoff tool, report requirements, or any mandatory post-event briefing process — those run first.

## The last forty minutes, in order

| Minutes | What happens | Where |
|---|---|---|
| −40 to −25 | Close the loose ends: board state checked, callback pad cleared or handed, audit numbers ready | Desk |
| −25 to −10 | Charge-to-charge handoff, structured and honest | Desk or quiet corner |
| −10 to −0 | The thank-down (chapter 3's closing huddle, done properly) + final stairs/floor walk | Floor |
| After | Five-minute personal debrief with the [debrief sheet](../worksheets/debrief-sheet.html) | Anywhere quiet |

The single most reliable first-time-charger mistake: starting handoff with eleven minutes of unanswered board work and zero notes. If you write nothing down all shift, you cannot meet the obligation of an incoming charge who is about to own your mess.

## Charge-to-charge handoff, the shape that works

Incoming chargers need **state, pressure, and people** — in that order, with times attached.

```text
[CHARGE-TO-CHARGE HANDOFF — 4 SENTENCES + 4 DETAILS]

1. "Board state: [occupied/open/waiters-waiting], as of [time]."
2. "Pressure: [what's stuck — e.g., two admissions lagging on paperwork,
   one room waiting on transport]."
3. "Staffing: [who you have, who you're short, who is float/oncall]."
4. "People to watch out for: [which assignments are heavy tonight and
   why — workload shape, never opinions about people]."
5. Details you should hand over: [admit board status, the amber/red
   escalations you made, any missed promises to callers, equipment/
   facility issues still open].
6. "The one thing I'd want to know before you sit down: [...]."
```

Same rule as family calls: **hand what you promised you would.** If a caller is owed a callback from your shift and it has not happened, say so in sentence 5 by name and time. Handoff is not an exit interview; it is where your open loops become someone else's clear plan — or, if you hid them, next shift's ambush.

> **A handoff honesty example, observed style:** two chargers in a row. The first finished the shift and reported clean numbers with one professional mention: "One callback that got missed at 14:00, office already knows" — second charger started the shift with the whole picture and got credit for a smooth handoff they did not even have to earn. The shift before had essentially hidden it, and the second charge had to learn it from a voicemail. Same board, opposite trust.

## The thank-down, done properly

Chapter 3 gave you the script; here is what actually separates good thank-downs:

- **Name one specific observable thing** ("the way everyone covered the transport gap without being asked"), never generic praise.
- **Name it to the whole group,** then catch two people by name individually as they head out if you can — carpe the two seconds: "That grid save at 10:00 — thanks." You will be thanked eventually. Start the chain yourself.
- **Do not turn it into an evaluation.** Thank-downs are not feedback; nobody is coached at the door. Save real coaching for another day, or say "grab a coffee this week, I want to trade notes."

"You owe nobody a thank-down." Yes you do. A team that never hears a specific thank-you decides the desk does not notice them — and then the desk stops getting told things, which is how chargers go blind.

## The five-minute personal debrief

This is the part built for you alone, and it is where the role actually becomes yours. Use the [debrief sheet](../worksheets/debrief-sheet.html) on blank paper, four questions:

1. **State:** what was the board's worst moment, and did the audit numbers predict it?
2. **Ask:** what did I escalate — one thing I escalated *earlier than felt comfortable*, and one thing I *held too long*?
3. **Fair:** what did the grid look like in hindsight? One assignment I would re-trade, and the reason I would give out loud if I did.
4. **Next:** one change I would make next charged shift. One. Single. Change.

The rule that keeps debriefs alive: **one change per shift.** Chargers who write five changes inherit five things to feel bad about and abandon the entire practice by week three. One change compounds.

## When the shift ended badly

Some charged shifts contain a hard moment — a failure to save someone, a family vigil, an event that shakes the floor. The thank-down and personal debrief still run, but they are not enough. In that case:

- Suggest and support your facility's formal debriefing process, the one your facility defines; you do not have to run it yourself — your job is opening the door for it, not leading clinical review.
- Say something at handoff even if it is small: "The floor had a hard one today" — the next charger needs to know *that* to walk the door kindly.
- Do the personal debrief anyway, but only the questions that feel possible. What you write down is yours.
- And later, off the floor: the same people who support you as a nurse support you as a charger. Reach for them. Charged-form or bedside-form, the hard nights take the same amount of human being.

## The one-line summary

Handoff with state-pressure-people plus your open loops, thank one specific thing to one specific group, and one single change per shift in a five-minute debrief — this forty minutes is how a charger is made.
