# Flow and Bed Math

**Education only — this field manual is never medical advice.** This chapter teaches the counting arithmetic chargers live in: how many rooms turn over, how much pressure is behind the board, and when to sound the alarm. It uses toy unit numbers only, and never overrides your facility's capacity protocols, bed board rules, or house supervisor's decisions.

## Your shift, expressed as one equation

Every shift is a race between two queues:

> **Rooms opening** (discharges, transfers out) versus **people waiting** (admits, holds, transfers in).

Everything the charge desk does — pushing discharges, chasing housekeeping, passing on holds, brokering with support staff — is one of those two queues getting pushed. Once you think in queues, the board stops being furniture and starts being a rate.

## The three numbers to keep in your head

Rule of thumb for a twelve-hour day shift, on a unit of 30 staffed rooms (use your unit's real numbers — these are a teaching shell):

| Number | The toy version | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Openings by mid-shift** | "6 of my 30 rooms have discharge or transfer-out plans before 12:00" | This is your morning fuel. Until openings land, you cannot accept work. |
| **Weighted occupancy** | Total census ÷ staffed beds — but weight the *high-acuity* rooms heavier | 28/30 with 4 heavy rooms is more pressure than 30/30 balanced |
| **Waiter pressure** | "3 holds in house plus 2 expected ER admits" | Every waiter is a clock: they started somewhere else's shift, and they age |

## The mid-morning audit (takes four minutes at 10:00)

Once a shift, before the lunch wave:

1. Walk the whiteboard (or bed board). Write three numbers: rooms still occupied with discharge orders *not yet* written, rooms that are physically clean and open, waiters in house.
2. Compare against the admit board. If waiters > open-clean rooms, your queue is backing up and somebody has to be pushed: physicians to write orders, case management to place, transport to run, housekeeping to turn.
3. If orders-not-yet-written is the bottleneck, that is your escalation target for the hour. (See [when to escalate](06-when-to-escalate.html).)

**How it usually goes, observed style:**

> At 10:00 the board shows seven "ready for discharge" rooms, no open rooms, two holds waiting. You do the math out loud at the desk: "Seven turn-overs get me open, and I need four open by noon to take what's coming. I'm three short." Now the ask has a number attached, and "we need to move faster" becomes "I need two discharges processed and one escort, please." Numbers turn requests into work.

## Discharges are the chargers' job

Nobody teaches this to first-time chargers: **your job is not to *do* the discharge work, your job is to make it happen on schedule.** The routine:

- Post the discharge wave in the huddle: who leaves before 10, who leaves before noon, which one is the "wait on ride or paperwork" one.
- Every hour you are at the desk, chase the wave in one pass: "Room 8 left? Housekeeping asked?" That is the whole call.
- The moment a room closes, its clock is running — your next huddle-level sentence is "we just opened one."

Do not let a single discharge dangle for an hour because the right person was busy. Dangling rooms are how boards clog, and clogged boards are how you end up with waiters who age into last shift's guests.

## Using the bed math in one honest sentence

You will eventually be asked by a supervisor, mid-shift, "can you take more?" The honest answer is a five-sentence version of your audit:

> "I have [waiters] in house and [open-clean] rooms. My openings by noon count [this many] if [this one thing] moves. I can take [one] more safely. I can't take more than that until [one bottleneck] happens."

That sentence turns a push into arithmetic — which is the only defense you have from the desk that survives the day.

## When the math says no and reality says anyway

Some nights you will be told the decision has been made and the waiters are coming regardless. What you do next is climb the escalation chain (chapter 6) — not agree out loud and quietly swell the board. Document the timeline and what was asked, and keep pushing for the resources behind the decision. "The math is bad, I said so, resources were requested" is a survivable sentence at debrief. A silently-stretched board is not.

## The one-line summary

Chargers think in two queues and three numbers; your job at the desk is not more work — it is making other people's scheduled work land on time, out loud, with the count attached.
