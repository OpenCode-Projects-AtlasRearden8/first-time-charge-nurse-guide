# Family Call Scripts

**Education only — this field manual is never medical advice.** This chapter covers the phone calls chargers field: updates, complaints, "just checking in," and the hard ones. It teaches communication patterns only — never clinical status, never diagnoses, and it never overrides your facility's privacy policy or the care team's plan about who may receive what information.

## The charger's first phone rule

You are the desk, not the person, and you pass clinical calls to the room:

> "Clinical details belong with the nurse or provider caring for that person. Let me connect you."

But you are *great* at everything else — logistics, timing, who is where, how the shift is staffed. Fielding the non-clinical so the room nurses can give report in peace is part of your job. The trick is knowing which is which, fast.

## The two-sentence intake for any incoming call

Before anything, take control in the same two sentences every time:

```text
"Charge desk, [first name or 'it's the charge desk'], can I get a number
where I'll call you back if we get disconnected?"

"Let me get the person's first-name-only and which room you're asking about,
and I'll see what I can tell you."
```

Asking for a callback number up front is not paranoia — it is insurance against a mid-call drop, and it costs three seconds.

## Script 1 — the routine update request

The most common call of the shift.

> "Thanks for checking in. I can tell you where things stand schedule-wise: they're on the plan we discussed this morning [or: I don't have a schedule change to report]. Anything about their medical care, I'd have to connect the nurse — want me to do that, or is the schedule piece enough today?"

Two things make it work: you give the *logistics* answer (which is yours to give), and you offer the handoff instead of refusing it. Nobody has to feel shooed away.

## Script 2 — "why hasn't anyone called me?"

This call is usually frustration, not accusation. It is also often true: the room nurse got busy. Do not defend, do not scold the colleague in front of the caller.

> "I'm sorry — I'll find out and have someone call you back. Can I get your number? And is there a window that's better — right after dinner, or before lunch?"

Then *do it*, in writing on the desk pad: room, request, time, who is assigned to call back. The single biggest trust-builder at the desk is callbacks happening when promised.

## Script 3 — the angry caller

Anger on the phone is almost always about fear wearing armor. Your only goals: lower the temperature, route it forward, never absorb it personally.

```text
1.  "I hear you — that would be frustrating for me too."
2.  "Here's what I can do from the desk: [one concrete thing]."
3.  "Here's who can do more: [manager / patient advocate / provider office]."
    "I can walk this over to them right now myself if you'd like."
```

Never match volume, never editorialize about colleagues ("yeah they should have..."), never promise outcomes. One concrete thing you will do — then have done — is worth ten apologetic sentences.

**A how-it-usually-goes, observed style:**

> A caller is upset about a wait, and is three sentences from crying. You do not fix the wait. You say "I hear you, I want to help with the part I can: let me check where things stand and call you back in twenty minutes, with your number." Twenty-eight minutes later you call back — even a partial update called *late but promised* rebuilds more trust than an instant fix.

## Script 4 — the "can I speak to whoever is in charge" call

That is you. Say so plainly, without the defensive laugh.

> "That's me right now — I'm the charge desk. What can I help with?"

Then run the intake. If the topic turns clinical, route it; if it is about the unit (parking, timing, staffing, when visiting hours resume after an event), it is yours to answer or mine to escalate to the manager.

## Script 5 — the vigil situation (goodbyes)

Former unit-mates told me the hardest version of this call is the quiet one: a family calling to plan arrival, coordinate people, ask whether the room is private, ask when others can come. Patterns that hold:

- Slow down your speaking rate. Half speed, full kindness.
- Logistics are yours: parking, timing, who can come, what the waiting area looks like from your desk.
- Offer the handoff for the medical conversation: "The nurse with them right now can walk you through the rest — I'll connect you."
- Then connect them. A warm transfer — "I have you with me, I'm passing you to [room]" — beats a cold line every time.

Nothing on this page tells a caller anything clinical. If you don't have the medical conversation, the scripts above are whole.

## Callback hygiene

The desk pad is a legal-adjacent tool. Keep it bare: room identifier, caller's callback number, exactly one line about the request, and the initials of who took it. No clinical notes, no personal commentary — it lives in a shared space and anyone at the desk may be the one who answers next.

## The one-line summary

Own the logistics, offer the clinical handoff, promise the callback and fire it — the phone is where chargers quietly earn (or never earn) the floor's trust.
