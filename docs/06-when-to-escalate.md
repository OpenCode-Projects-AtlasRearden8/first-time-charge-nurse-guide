# When to Escalate

**Education only — this field manual is never medical advice.** This chapter teaches when to call for help and who to call — a communication and role-planning skill. It does not define clinical conditions or codes, never replaces your facility's escalation policy (rapid response rules, chain of command, on-call directories), and any clinical judgment about a person's condition stays with the room nurse and the care team.

## The escalation reflex has to be trained before you need it

The single most common first-time-charger failure is *escalating late* — not because chargers are careless, but because escalation feels like confessing you could not handle the role. Flip it in your head: **escalation is a charger's best resource, used on purpose, and it works better early than late.** A call that arrives with five hours of runway fixes a problem; the same call at 22:00 becomes a rescue.

## The three-level trigger card

Sort every situation into green, amber, or red based on *who has to move*, not how you feel about it:

| Level | The situation's shape | Who gets told | Clock |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Green** | The plan can absorb it; you fix it inside your unit | No one outside; just do it or route to who can | None |
| **Amber** | The plan is now bending: order lag, dead room clock, staffing gap, first late callback | The owner of the bottleneck, with a name and a time attached | Same hour |
| **Red** | The plan is broken and will hurt something if you wait: care question nobody on-site can answer, covering the desk means nobody can answer a room, staffing below what the shift can cover | House supervisor, manager, and whoever owns the resource — all named in your facility policy, using your facility's channels | Now |

Amber is where first-time chargers live too long. The fix is a rule: **after your second attempt at an amber, it becomes red.** If you have asked the same thing of the same person twice and the clock is still running, you are not asking anymore — you are waiting, and waiting is not a charger's job.

## The escalation sentence (use the same shape every time)

Escalations work when they are short, factual, and end in a single ask:

```text
"Charge desk on [unit]. I have [the count from the audit — waiters in house,
 rooms not turning, staffing gap by hours]. I've already tried [the two
 things]. What I need is [one specific resource or decision]. Can I ask you
 for that, or take it to [next in the chain]?"
```

Notice three features. The count comes first (numbers make it real). Your two attempts are named (proves it is not a first reflex). The ask is one sentence about one thing.

**How it usually goes, observed style:**

> An escort is lagging 90 minutes, and you have two holds waiting on that room. Phone the transport lead: "Charge on 7. My audit: two waiters waiting on rooms, one escort call out since 09:40, no movement yet. Already called twice. I need either that escort re-prioritized or a second chair now. Which can I get?" Compare the useless version: "transport seems slow today." Same fact, zero traction.

## The chain, practiced before you need it

By the end of your first charge week, fill in three blanks *from your actual facility*, not from memory of somebody else's unit:

1. **Desk-visible resource** for the most common amber issues (order lag, room-turn lag) — name them in your own unit's terms.
2. **The assistant/lead, the house supervisor, and the manager** — with *numbers you actually have*, written on the [escalation card](../worksheets/escalation-card.html), checked and refreshed each shift.
3. **The after-hours version of facts** — who is on call, what the call schedule name is, where the number lives.

First-time chargers lose hours discovering #2 and #3 *during* their first red moment. Spend the fifteen minutes on shift one. It is the cheapest fifteen minutes of the year.

## The clinical-adjacent call you will be asked to make

You will be at the desk when someone says "I need a second look in here" — and you will feel the pull to step in yourself. Two different moves:

- **Resource move (always yours):** "I'm pulling the second body now — here's who's coming." Getting people to a room is charger work 100% of the time.
- **Clinical move (belongs to the room):** assessment, judgment, and orders belong to the people at the bedside. Your job at the desk is to make sure the *right* people at the bedside exist immediately.

Confusing these two is how charge roles quietly drift into substituted judgment. You deliver people; clinicians deliver care.

## Talking to your own team about escalation

Tell your floor nurses the same three levels once in the huddle:

> "Flag red to me anytime — that's what I'm for. Amber, come find me after two tries at it. If it's someone's turn to have the room covered while they go escalate, I make the call to cover it."

Declaring a no-fault escalation culture is a coverage promise that gets used. If your first "I need the room covered so I can call" comes back with you not covering — you will never hear amber again.

## The one-line summary

Three levels, the second-attempt rule, one sentence ending in one ask, and the chain written on a card before shift one — escalation is a skill you rehearse, not a confession you make.
