# Assignment Fairness

**Education only — this field manual is never medical advice.** This chapter covers how to build a patient assignment that is fair, safe, and explainable. It does not replace your facility's staffing plan, acuity tool, or union/contract assignments — where those exist, they are the rulebook and you run them first.

## Fair is not equal

The grid you are about to build will never have identical workloads. A fair assignment is one where **nobody can look at it and reasonably say "why me?"** — which is a different target than identical.

The three inputs a first-time charger has:

1. **Head requirements:** it is 1:5 on med-surg, 1:4 on step-down, 1:3 on your unit's plan — whatever your facility's ratios and skill-mix say.
2. **Heads you have:** number and *level* of nurses (new grad, six months, float, agency).
3. **The board:** total-care telemetry rooms, standby holds, expected discharges, the two admits waiting.

## The grid-building drill, line by line

Build it in this order every single time. Same order = same trust.

**Step 1 — lock the fixed points.** Some rooms go to a specific skill level no matter what: a high-acuity pair stays with the most senior pair; a new-to-unit nurse does not get the standby-hold pair alone. Write these first, in ink.

**Step 2 — sort rooms by expected work, not by census count.** Two five-room assignments are not the same five-room assignment. Quick scoring you can do in your head:

| Room signals more work when... | Reasonable adjustment |
|---|---|
| Admission expected to land this shift | Trade for a room with a discharge leaving early |
| A patient needs transport with escort each time it moves | Pair the room near a teammate with open windows |
| The plan for the day is "battle the board" (chase stays, wait on Saturday-surges) | Give the nurse a lighter side-assignment than the neighbor gets |
| Family/interpreter-heavy coordination | Recognize it in your head — heavy phone days are heavy |

**Step 3 — co-sign the pairs, not the rooms.** When a room shows "this will need a second body at some point," name the second body on the grid *ahead of time*: "Rooms 12 and 15 co-tenanted — anyone with a window covers either." Neighbors who are pre-assigned backup fight less when they are finally called.

**Step 4 — say the trade out loud.** Fairness is invisible until you narrate it. Twenty seconds per swap:

> "I gave you the admit because your third room discharges at 10 — even swap. Anyone want it smoother, say so before I post this."

That sentence does what eight hours of silent adjustment never will.

## The floating and agency question

Floats and agency staff are not lesser nurses — they are different-location nurses. Fairness here has two half-sentences:

- **Give the float a predictable unit-effort assignment** — similar types of rooms to their home unit when you can. Predictability is what they need to be useful today.
- **Say the same rules to everyone.** If the float is capped at step-down types of rooms per policy, enforce it for everyone, not selectively — and say what falls where so no one can claim it's personal.

**A how-it-usually-goes fairness argument, observed style:**

> Two friends were assigned apart and one argues the assignment should have paired them. It is 06:45. You have about a minute. "I hear it. The grid had to sit two different skill levels on that side. Swap is possible — it puts an admit on the second nurse, so I need both of you to say yes before I redo it." Framing it as a consequence, not a ruling, ends the argument nine times out of ten.

## The both-sides ledger

Every grumbling nurse eventually asks a version of "did *you* have to work?" So your grid needs two sides, equally filled:

- **Off-sides duties:** the grid's non-room work that a first-time charger ends up carrying — the unit secretary's 10:00 breaks, the admission log, calling the standby-hold rooms, checking the care-escort plan.
- **On-sides duties:** the room work.

If your team sees the desk load and the room load written down together, they see a person, not a vacancy. If you under-report your own load, expect the low simmer to turn into "charge has no clue."

## Redo or not?

You will be asked to re-do assignments mid-shift. Decide with the three-question test:

1. Has a *new fact* existed since you posted the grid (sick call, patient need, staffing change)? If no, do not redo for comfort — redo for cause.
2. Is the requested swap neutral for the board (nobody else gets hurt)? If yes, redo is cheap.
3. Does redoing it create another unfair assignment? Fairness is a whole-grid property, not a one-line fix.

## The one-line summary

Fairness is a published trade with your reasons narrated, fixed points locked first, and the desk work counted out loud with the room work — not a promise that the work will ever be equal.
