# Weathering the Storm

**Education only — this field manual is never medical advice.** This chapter covers the mid-shift events first-time chargers dread: sick calls, sudden surges, and the two hours that decide the shift. It does not replace your facility's call-out policy, staffing office procedures, or the chain of command — your policies run first.

## The sick call at 06:10

How it usually goes:

> 06:10, an hour before the shift: a staffer texts that they will not be in. The grid you were going to build is now one body short. Everything from here is arithmetic plus a phone.

Your sequence, in the exact order that avoids wasted motion:

1. **Rework the grid on paper first, before any call.** "If nobody comes, what is the least-bad grid?" You may be able to staff it acceptable-but-tight for two hours until the day's expected openings land.
2. **Then call for coverage** in this order per your facility's process: the staffing office/pool, the on-call list, the house supervisor. Use your facility's channels — the ones named in your policy, not the friendliest one.
3. **Then decide whether the assembly plan holds or you need escalation.** If the answer is "acceptable-but-tight for the full twelve," say it out loud in your huddle with the count attached: "We are one short. Here's the rework. Here's the consequence: [what will run late]. Here's what I want flagged to me: [the amber triggers]."

Never absorb the absence silently — your team can live with a rework; what poisons a shift is a rework nobody announced until 15:00.

## The surge lands anyway

Board lurch: several admissions land at once while your discharges are still processing. Your tools, in order:

1. **Micro-huddle** (chapter 3's 60-second version): one state, one change, one ask.
2. **Re-run the audit** (chapter 4): waiters in house, open-clean rooms, openings by noon. Numbers first, panic never.
3. **Push the friendly bottleneck.** Surges usually unblock on the *simplest* lever: one discharge that has been waiting on a ride or a signature for 40 minutes.
4. **Then escalate** with the audit sentence from chapter 6. Surges that exceed your capacity become house supervisor decisions *fast* — bringing them early is efficient, not weak.

## The two hours that decide the shift

On an average 12-hour day shift the high-pressure window is predictable: the block where openings will not happen again until evening, waiters are at their oldest, and lunch is due. In that window, first-time chargers make three signature mistakes:

- **Dropping the callback pad.** During the two hours, callbacks are the first thing to fall behind — and the loudest at the family level (chapter 5).
- **Sitting down.** The desk chair in that window belongs to whoever is doing one thing; a sitting charger is a charger not watching the board.
- **Going hands-on into the surge.** Your hands are the least useful hands in the building during the surge — they are routing anyone else's hands.

A **2-hour audit cadence** instead of hourly in the surge is worth borrowing: run the three numbers every 120 minutes, out loud, no matter how the shift feels.

## The second body you forgot you have

Chargers under pressure forget that they grant themselves help. Resources you always control:

- **Cancelling the non-urgent niceties.** The unit-recognition meeting, the carry-over project, the extra room polish — swept.
- **Calling in your leads, your eyes, and the float.** Asking a senior nurse to "take the desk while I call" is charger behavior, not weakness; it was always part of your reach (chapter 1).
- **Making the red call.** In chapter 6's terms, a shift that has become unacceptable is discovered fast here, not silently endured until handoff.

**A how-it-usually-goes, observed style:**

> One call-out, a surge with three holds, and a missing escort, all before noon. The new charger toughed the whole thing quietly until 18:00 handoff — the senior they should have asked an hour after the surge never got called, the callback pad ran four hours behind and two phone complaints landed at 17:00. Same shift, same facts run by a charger who asked at noon for a second desk chair and stuck to the audit cadence, ends expecting a manageable debrief. The difference is not skill. It is whether the charger asked.

## Protecting yourself during the storm

You can feel two ways and be one useful thing at once. Mid-storm habits:

- **The 90-second sink reset.** Any time you can, stop in the med-room doorframe, breathe once-down, drink, back out. Small, real, and yours.
- **Do not eat your lunch at the desk.** Do it a bit late if so. Desk-eating chargers discover by 16:00 that they hand off shaky.
- **Watch-fors in your own head:** snappish sentence at the desk, unaware of last 30 minutes of the board, dread at the phone ring. Any one shows and it is time for the 90-second reset.

## After the storm, at handoff

Storm shifts deserve the full debrief treatment from [chapter 8](08-end-of-shift-debrief.html) — what was asked late, what was nearly missed, and who to thank. Storms are where your next storm comes from: a debri-reported near-miss closes the gap; an unmentioned one guarantees the same gap next time.

## The one-line summary

The storm is arithmetic plus a phone: rework the grid before you call, micro-huddle first, numbers before panic, and ask for help at the two-hours mark instead of absorbing at the desk until 18:00.
