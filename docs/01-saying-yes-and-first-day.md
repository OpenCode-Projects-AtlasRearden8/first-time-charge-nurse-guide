# Saying Yes and the First Day

**Education only — this field manual is never medical advice.** This chapter covers the moment you are asked to take charge, what to confirm before you say yes, and your first hour at the desk. It does not replace your facility's charge-nurse policy, staffing plan, or orientation process — where those exist, they win.

## The ask: what usually happens

Nobody hands you a binder and a week's notice. It goes like this:

> Your manager catches you at 14:00: "You've been on this unit two years. Can you take charge tomorrow — just for a bit, while we cover a vacancy?"

Or, the night-shift version:

> House supervisor calls at 18:30: "We need a charge for 7-north. You're the senior one scheduled. You up for it?"

Two things are true at once: you are flattered, and you are worried the word "no" ends something. It does not. Watch how both answers work.

## The one-minute confirmation (before you say yes)

Ask three questions. You can string them into one sentence.

> "Sure — can you tell me what's actually expected? Who covers the desk if I'm still running a room? And who do I call if it goes sideways tonight?"

You are confirming three facts:

| Question | What you want to learn |
|---|---|
| What is expected? | Is charge a full desk role, or charge-with-patients? Does yours come with a reduced assignment? |
| Who backs me up? | A co-charge? The floating team lead at 19:00? House supervisor? |
| What is the unit's process? | Does your facility expect a written assignment sheet, a huddle, a bed board update, or anything signed? |

If the answer to "what if it goes sideways" is vague — that is not a no-signal. It is a signal that *you* define the fallback tonight: house supervisor, manager's number, on-call group. Write the names down before you arrive.

> **Trap to avoid:** saying yes by silent omission. If you never confirm the role out loud, expect to be handed a full (not reduced) assignment and *also* the desk. Confirm out loud, even if it costs an audible sigh.

## The first hour at the desk

You will feel a pull to "start being helpful" immediately. Resist it. The first sixty minutes buy the whole shift.

**Minute 0–15: count what you actually have.**

- How many staff nurses, and who is new-to-practice or floating?
- How many aids/techs, transport, and unit secretary?
- How many rooms: staffed, blocked, dirty?
- What is on the admit board, and what is on the discharge list?
- Who called in, and what is the expected census by mid-shift?

**Minute 15–30: build the assignment grid** (chapter 2 walks it line by line).

**Minute 30–45: run the huddle** (chapter 3 has the script).

**Minute 45–60: walk the floor visibly once.** Say the same sentence to every group: *"Here's the grid, here's what's coming, find me if it's not working."* Being seen once early prevents being hunted all night.

## Your shift is now the unit's shift

The mental flip nobody warns you about: your room no longer exists (or exists smaller). The unit is your room. When a staff nurse says "I need you in here," your old reflex says you go. Your new job says *triage first*: does the request need your hands, or your *reach* — a resource, an order round, an extra pair already standing four doors down?

**A hands-versus-reach example, how it usually goes:**

> Room situation needs two people for twenty minutes. You are at the desk with admit paperwork open. Instead of sprinting, one look around tells you an aide-with-no-wait and a walking-glass-float are thirty feet away. You route them, then go yourself only if it is still lean. Total delay: fifteen seconds, and the desk never stopped.

This is not about never helping. It is about helping *last* instead of *first*, so the whole floor keeps moving instead of the one room you happened to be in.

## If you were asked overnight and you said yes in a daze

Happens constantly. Recovery plan:

1. Arrive thirty minutes early. The grid and huddle still work.
2. Tell your manager-in-name (or house supervisor) it is your first desk shift, same sentence you tell the team.
3. Keep the [escalation card](../worksheets/escalation-card.html) next to the desk phone.

## The one-line summary

The ask will feel like a demotion to scare and a promotion to safety — it is neither. Confirm loud, count first, grid second, huddle third, then walk the floor like it is yours, because it is.
